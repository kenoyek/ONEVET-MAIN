The morning flock check: five clues your chickens need attention

Before you reach for the feed scoop, pause at the entrance to your poultry house. What do you hear? Where are the birds standing? Are they moving towards you, or is a quiet group staying behind?

A short daily observation routine helps you notice changes while you still have time to investigate. It cannot diagnose disease, but it can give your veterinarian a much clearer picture when something is wrong.

1. Listen before you walk in

Get familiar with the usual sounds of your flock. New coughing, sneezing, unusual silence or noisy breathing deserves attention. Watch several birds at rest rather than judging only those disturbed by your arrival. A bird struggling to breathe needs prompt veterinary assessment.

2. Follow the water

Check that clean drinking water is available and that drinkers actually work. A full tank does not guarantee water is reaching the last drinker. Look for blocked nipples, leaking fittings and competition around too few drinking points. Clean equipment regularly and position it to limit contamination from litter.

In hot weather, check water access more often. Panting, wings held away from the body and crowding in cooler areas can signal heat stress, although illness can cause similar signs. Improve shade and appropriate ventilation, maintain water access and avoid unnecessary handling during the hottest hours. Keep electrical equipment safely installed and have a plan for power interruptions.

3. Watch the birds that do not join in

Healthy activity varies with age, weather and time of day. What matters is a change from your flock's usual pattern: birds hanging back, looking hunched, limping or losing interest in feed. Check feed for dampness, mould and contamination, and store it dry and protected from rodents. Do not simply increase feed to explain away a sudden change in appetite.

If you can safely separate an unwell bird, use a designated area with separate equipment and seek veterinary advice. Handle healthy birds first, then unwell birds, and wash your hands and clean footwear afterwards. Isolation alone does not rule out a flock-wide problem.

4. Look down at the litter

Wet patches often point to a leaking drinker or a ventilation problem. Remove wet material and address its cause rather than repeatedly covering it. Droppings vary with diet and normal digestion, so one unusual dropping is not a diagnosis. Repeated watery or bloody droppings, especially alongside weakness or reduced feeding, warrant veterinary advice.

Keep visitors and shared equipment to a minimum. Use dedicated footwear for the poultry area, protect feed and water from wild birds, and ask your veterinarian about a suitable separation period before introducing new birds. Cleaning away dirt must come before effective disinfection; follow the product label and never mix chemicals.

5. Let a simple record tell the story

Write down the date, flock age and size, feed and water observations, deaths, unusual signs and, for layers, egg numbers. A sudden fall in egg production can have several causes, including heat, feed changes and illness. Records help distinguish a one-off event from a developing pattern.

Take useful photographs or short videos without distressing the birds. Tell your veterinarian when the change started, how many birds are affected, and whether new birds, feed or visitors arrived recently.

When the routine becomes urgent

Unexpected deaths, several birds becoming ill together, severe breathing difficulty, inability to stand or a sudden major change in feed or water use need urgent veterinary advice. Stop moving birds, eggs and equipment off the premises until you receive guidance if a contagious disease is suspected. Your veterinarian or local animal-health authority can advise on reporting and safe handling. Do not sell or slaughter sick birds for food.

Avoid guessing with antibiotics or copying another farm's vaccination programme. A veterinarian should tailor prevention to your flock and local risks; any medicine must be appropriate and its egg and meat withdrawal instructions followed.

Try this tomorrow

Stand quietly, listen, check the water, watch the flock, inspect the litter and write one line in your notebook. Repeat at a consistent time. Knowing what normal looks like is one of the most useful tools a poultry keeper can build.
