Bored pet, busy home? Try this seven-day enrichment challenge

Your dog has brought you the same toy three times. Your cat has decided the curtain is a climbing frame. More activity may help, but enrichment is about more than tiring an animal out: it gives them safe opportunities to sniff, explore, solve problems and choose how to spend their time.

Try one small change each day this week. Keep sessions short, supervise new activities and let your pet walk away. These ideas are for dogs and cats; adapt them to your pet's age, health and preferences, and ask your veterinarian before changing activity for an animal with a medical condition.

Day 1: Take the sniffing route

For dogs, turn a familiar walk into a slower exploration. Let your dog investigate safe smells while remaining on a secure lead where needed. Choose cooler parts of the day in hot weather, carry water and avoid hot surfaces. A short, interesting outing may suit your dog better than a rushed march.

For indoor cats, offer a safe new scent on a washable cloth or place a plain cardboard box in a quiet spot. Skip essential oils, fragranced sprays and unknown plants. Curiosity should never require exposure to something potentially toxic.

Day 2: Make one meal a gentle puzzle

Use part of your pet's normal daily food allowance in a suitable feeding puzzle. Start easy so the food remains accessible. A dog might enjoy an appropriate slow feeder; a cat may prefer a few pieces of its usual dry food placed in several easy-to-reach locations. Keep other pets separate if food causes tension.

Supervise and remove damaged items. Avoid small detachable parts, sharp edges and materials your pet may swallow. If your pet guards food, becomes frustrated or has dietary or swallowing problems, ask your veterinarian for a safer approach. Puzzle feeding should not mean extra calories or a missed meal.

Day 3: Teach one tiny skill

Reward a simple behaviour your pet already offers: looking at you, stepping onto a mat or touching a target. Use praise, play or a small portion of their usual food. Mark the moment with a consistent word, then reward promptly. Stop while the game is still enjoyable.

Cats can learn through rewards too. Keep the goal easy and never force a paw or hold an animal in position. If either of you becomes frustrated, make the task simpler and try again later.

Day 4: Create a place to choose

Give your pet a comfortable retreat away from household traffic. A dog may enjoy a quiet bed; a cat may prefer an accessible elevated perch or a hiding box with a clear exit. Make sure furniture is stable and windows and balconies are secure. Children should learn that a resting pet is left undisturbed.

In homes with several cats, distribute resting areas, food, water and litter facilities so one animal cannot easily block another's access. More choice can reduce everyday tension.

Day 5: Play the way your pet plays

Offer your cat a wand toy that moves like prey, let them catch it, then put it away safely. String, ribbons and wand toys should not be left available unsupervised. Never encourage play with hands or feet.

For dogs, try a gentle game with a suitable toy and a clear pause cue. Avoid repetitive jumping or sudden turns for pets with joint problems. Watch the animal in front of you: enthusiasm does not make an activity suitable for every body.

Day 6: Practise comfortable handling

Let your pet approach voluntarily. Briefly touch an area they already tolerate, reward calm participation and stop before discomfort. This is preparation for everyday care, not a reason to inspect a painful ear, force open a mouth or restrain a frightened animal.

Turning away, freezing, hiding, growling or trying to leave are reasons to pause and give space. Never punish a warning. Persistent fear or handling difficulty deserves help from your veterinarian and an appropriately qualified behaviour professional.

Day 7: Let your pet vote

Offer two familiar, safe activities at different times and note what your pet chooses. Which one produces relaxed engagement? Which one leads to frustration or avoidance? Keep the favourites in a small rotation. You do not need a cupboard full of toys to make daily life more interesting.

When a behaviour change needs a health check

Sudden hiding, irritability, reduced appetite, unusual drinking, toileting changes or a loss of interest in play can indicate illness or pain. Do not assume your pet is simply bored. Arrange veterinary advice for a new or persistent change.

Difficulty breathing, collapse, suspected poisoning, repeated unproductive retching or straining without passing urine require urgent veterinary attention. Do not delay care to try an enrichment activity or give human medicines. Open-mouth breathing in a cat is also an emergency.

Your challenge for next week

Choose the two activities your pet enjoyed most and build them into a routine you can actually maintain. Keep fresh water, appropriate food, rest and veterinarian-guided preventive care at the centre of that routine. The aim is a pet with more opportunities to feel safe, interested and comfortable in your home.
